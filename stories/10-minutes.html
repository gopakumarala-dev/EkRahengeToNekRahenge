SOURCES & INTERPRETATIONS

HISTORY IS
BUILT FROM EVIDENCE.

Historical knowledge comes from many kinds of evidence. Written records, inscriptions, coins, architecture, archaeological remains, manuscripts and other surviving materials can help us reconstruct the past.

But a source is not automatically the same thing as a historical conclusion. We need to ask who produced it, when, why and what it can actually establish.

STEP 01

START WITH
THE CLAIM.

Every historical argument begins with a claim: something is said to have happened, existed, changed or continued.

Before accepting the claim, identify exactly what is being asserted. A precise claim can then be tested against the available evidence.

STEP 02 — FIND THE SOURCE

Look for the original or earliest available source whenever possible. A quotation copied through several later publications is not the same as examining the underlying document.

STEP 03

TRANSLATION
MATTERS.

Historical documents may have been written in languages and scripts that differ from those used today.

A translated passage should therefore be considered in relation to the original text, its terminology and its historical context.

STEP 04 — CONTEXT

A source does not exist in isolation. Its date, location, author, intended audience and purpose can influence what it tells us.

Context helps distinguish between what a source directly records and what later historians infer from it.

STEP 05

COMPARE
THE EVIDENCE.

Strong historical investigation rarely depends on a single piece of evidence. Different sources can confirm, qualify or challenge one another.

Archaeological evidence may illuminate a written account. An inscription may provide information that can be compared with literary or material evidence.

EVIDENCE ≠ INTERPRETATION

Historical evidence can support an interpretation, but the interpretation itself may involve reasoning, comparison and debate.

This distinction matters. A responsible historical presentation should make clear what the source directly says and what is being inferred from it.

THE KEY QUESTION

WHAT CAN WE
ACTUALLY ESTABLISH?

The goal is not simply to collect dramatic stories. The goal is to determine which claims are supported by evidence, which remain uncertain and where historians disagree.

That approach allows history to be investigated rather than merely repeated.
