01 / THE QUESTION

How do you tell
a history spanning
thousands of years?

Not as a list of dates. Not as a parade of rulers. And not as a story that begins in 1947.

We begin with the land and the people who inhabited it. We follow the rise of settlements and cities, Vedic traditions, the worlds remembered through Itihāsa, kingdoms and empires, philosophy and science, temples, maritime connections, invasions and resistance, colonial rule, the freedom struggle, Partition and the Republic.

We will celebrate achievement—and we will also look into the mirror. Where did disunity hurt us? Where did social exclusion weaken society? Where did rulers make disastrous choices? What did generations learn?

03 / DEEP ANTIQUITY

THE FIRST
STORIES ON STONE

Long before written chronicles, people were already leaving marks of their presence on the landscapes of Bharat.

Bhimbetka preserves rock shelters and paintings spanning prehistoric and historical periods. Here history begins with humility: a painting can reveal human activity and visual expression, but it cannot automatically tell us the complete beliefs or social structure of its makers.

Archaeology gives us fragments. The historian's task is to reconstruct the story without pretending that every unanswered question has already been solved.

DHOLAVIRA • GUJARAT
04 / THE FIRST GREAT URBAN STORY

WHEN
CITIES ROSE

Imagine entering a settlement more than four thousand years ago.

There are streets, houses, wells, workshops, fortifications and carefully engineered systems for collecting and managing water. Dholavira reveals an urban tradition capable of organising labour, materials, craft production and long-distance exchange.

UNESCO describes Dholavira as an exceptionally preserved Harappan settlement, occupied for roughly 1,500 years, with sophisticated water management and evidence of trade extending toward Mesopotamia and the Oman peninsula.

05 / THE VEDIC WORLD

WHEN KNOWLEDGE
WAS CARRIED BY MEMORY

The Vedic tradition opens a different window into early Bharat. Its history is not preserved only in excavated stone and pottery, but also through texts, language, ritual and oral transmission.

The Vedic corpus became part of a disciplined oral culture. Questions of chronology, geography and the relationship between textual and archaeological evidence remain subjects of serious study. We will present the evidence and the debates rather than flattening them into a single slogan.

From the Vedas to the Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads, the intellectual story develops into questions about dharma, reality, self, knowledge and liberation.

07 / THE AGE OF KNOWLEDGE

WHEN IDEAS
BECAME INSTITUTIONS

Indian intellectual history is much larger than a list of inventions. It is a history of questions and methods: how language works, how the heavens can be measured, how illness can be understood, how arguments can be tested and how human beings should live.

Panini, Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, Charaka, Sushruta and many other figures belong to different periods and traditions. Each story must be anchored to texts, manuscripts, inscriptions and scholarship.

Nalanda gives another international window: UNESCO describes it as a major monastic and scholastic institution whose organised transmission of knowledge continued for centuries and influenced institutions across South and Southeast Asia.

08 / TEMPLE CIVILISATION

WHEN
STONE SPOKE

Across centuries, temple building became one of the great expressions of Bharatiya civilisation. Architecture, sculpture, ritual, music, patronage, inscription and community life met within sacred spaces.

The Great Living Chola Temples are recognised by UNESCO for outstanding achievements in architecture, sculpture, painting and bronze casting. Their worship traditions continue today.

10 / THE CHANGING MAP

HOW DID WE
ARRIVE AT TODAY'S INDIA?

Political geography changed repeatedly. Empires expanded and contracted; kingdoms rose and disappeared; colonial rule imposed new administrative structures; Partition created a new international frontier; the Republic later reorganised its internal states.

We must distinguish civilisational geography from political sovereignty. That distinction is essential when telling the story of Bharatvarsha.

12 / VIJAYANAGARA

A CITY OF
TEMPLES & EMPIRE

Hampi is not merely a ruin. It is the surviving landscape of a major South Indian imperial centre—temples, markets, water systems, fortifications and monumental architecture together telling a story of political and cultural ambition.

Its destruction and survival also give us a lesson in historical memory: a city can fall, while the civilisation that created and remembered it continues.

HAMPI • VIJAYANAGARA
13 / SWARAJYA

THE RISE OF
SWARAJYA

Shivaji Maharaj's story belongs to the larger history of political transformation in early modern Bharat: forts, administration, military organisation, diplomacy, coronation and the idea of Swarajya.

The Maratha story did not end with one ruler. It developed into a wider political force and changed the balance of power across large parts of the subcontinent.

The finished article will use contemporary records, inscriptions, letters, administrative material and modern scholarship to reconstruct the story in detail.
